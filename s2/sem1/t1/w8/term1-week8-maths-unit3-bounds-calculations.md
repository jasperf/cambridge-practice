## Section A &middot; Warm-up: Quick Recap: Bounds of One Number

**Method** for a measurement: find the unit of rounding, halve it, then go that far below (lower bound) and above (upper bound) the rounded value. Every number on this sheet is a measurement, so the upper bound is the halfway point itself.

**Example:** 6 cm to the nearest cm → half a unit is 0.5 → **5.5 ≤ x < 6.5**.

### Questions

**Q1** (1 mark)
A mass is 3.4 kg, correct to 1 decimal place. What is its **lower** bound?

**Q2** (1 mark)
A length is 120 cm, correct to the nearest 10 cm. What is its **upper** bound?

**Q3** (1 mark)

- A) 8.1 ≤ *t* < 8.3
- B) 8.0 ≤ *t* < 8.4
- C) 8.15 ≤ *t* < 8.25
- D) 8.2 ≤ *t* < 8.3

**Q4** (1 mark)

## Section B: Several Identical Items

**Method:** to find the bounds of *n* identical items, find the bounds of **one** item first, then **multiply both bounds by *n***. Do not multiply the rounded value and then add the same ± as for one item: the error gets *n* times bigger too.

**Example:** 3 pencils, each 14 cm to the nearest cm. One pencil: 13.5 ≤ *x* < 14.5. Three pencils: 3 × 13.5 = 40.5 and 3 × 14.5 = 43.5, so **40.5 ≤ *x* < 43.5**. The total is 42 ± 1.5, not 42 ± 0.5.

**Writing the inequality:** the lower bound goes on the left with **≤**, and the upper bound on the right with **<**. Write the < sign clearly: it should not look like a 7 or a 1. A bound line such as 5.75 ≤ *x* < 6.25 should read out loud as "*x* is 5.75 or more, and less than 6.25".

## Section C: Adding and Subtracting Bounds

**Method:** write down the lower and upper bound of *each* number first, then decide which pairing gives the answer you want.

| Calculation | Largest possible answer | Smallest possible answer |
|---|---|---|
| a + b | UB(a) + UB(b) | LB(a) + LB(b) |
| a − b | UB(a) − LB(b) | LB(a) − UB(b) |

**Why the swap?** To make a *difference* as big as possible, start as high as you can and take away as little as you can. Subtracting flips the job of the second number.

**Example:** 7 and 3, both to the nearest whole number. Bounds: 6.5–7.5 and 2.5–3.5. Largest sum 7.5 + 3.5 = **11**. Largest difference 7.5 − 2.5 = **5**.

## Section D: Multiplying and Dividing Bounds

| Calculation | Largest possible answer | Smallest possible answer |
|---|---|---|
| a × b | UB(a) × UB(b) | LB(a) × LB(b) |
| a ÷ b | UB(a) ÷ LB(b) | LB(a) ÷ UB(b) |

**Why the swap for dividing?** Dividing by a *small* number gives a *big* answer (10 ÷ 2 = 5 but 10 ÷ 5 = 2). So the bottom number works the opposite way to the top one.

**Example:** a rectangle 7 cm by 3 cm, to the nearest cm. Largest area 7.5 × 3.5 = **26.25 cm²**. Smallest area 6.5 × 2.5 = **16.25 cm²**. The area is *not* 21 ± 0.5.

## Section E: Real Life: Will It Fit? Is It Safe?

**Method:** to decide whether something *definitely* fits or is safe, compare the **worst case**. Work out the largest the object could be and the smallest the space could be, then compare those two. If the worst case still works, it always works.

**Tip for several identical items:** use the bound of *one* item and multiply by how many there are. Eight people of 70 kg to the nearest 10 kg are at most 8 × 75 kg.

### Questions

**Q29** (1 mark)
Two identical shelves are each 1.5 m long, correct to 1 decimal place. They are placed end to end. What is the **upper** bound of their total length?

**Q30** (1 mark)
The gap in a wall is 3.2 m wide, correct to 1 decimal place. Will the two shelves from Q29 **definitely** fit?
- A) Yes: the longest the shelves could be (3.1 m) is less than the narrowest the gap could be (3.15 m)
- B) No: the gap could be as narrow as 3.15 m and the shelves as long as 3.2 m
- C) Cannot tell: 3.1 and 3.2 are too close together
- D) Yes, because 1.5 + 1.5 = 3.0, which is less than 3.2

**Q31** (2 marks)
A lift carries 8 people, each of mass 70 kg, correct to the nearest 10 kg. What is the **greatest** total mass of the 8 people?

**Q32** (1 mark)
Same lift, same 8 people. What is the **smallest** their total mass could be?

**Q33** (2 marks)
A stone has mass 240 g, correct to the nearest 10 g, and volume 40 cm³, correct to the nearest 10 cm³. What is the **greatest** possible density? *(density = mass ÷ volume)*

**Q34** (2 marks)
A field is 8.2 m by 5.4 m, each correct to 1 decimal place. What is the **upper** bound of its perimeter?
