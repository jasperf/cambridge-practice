## Section A: From Atoms to Ions

### Key facts

- **Why atoms react:** Every atom “wants” a full outer shell. The noble gases (Group 8) already have one, so they hardly react. Other atoms lose, gain or share electrons to get there.
- **What an ion is:** A charged particle made when an atom loses or gains *electrons*. The protons never change, so the element stays the same.
- **Which way?:** Metals have few outer electrons and **lose** them → positive ions. Non-metals have nearly full outer shells and **gain** electrons → negative ions.
- **The charge from the group:** Groups 1, 2, 3 → 1+, 2+, 3+ · Groups 5, 6, 7 → 3−, 2−, 1− · Group 4 and Group 8 do not usually form ions.
- **Counting electrons in an ion:** Start from the atomic number (protons). A + charge means that many electrons fewer; a − charge means that many more. Example: Li⁺ has 3 protons and 2 electrons.
- **Naming ions:** A metal ion keeps its name: lithium → lithium ion. A non-metal ion ends in **-ide**: bromine → bromide, phosphorus → phosphide.

## Section B: Writing Ionic Formulae

### Key facts

- **The one rule:** An ionic compound has no overall charge: total + charge = total − charge.
- **Step by step:** 1. Write each ion’s charge. · 2. Find the smallest number both charges go into. · 3. Use enough of each ion to reach it.
- **Worked example:** Al³⁺ and S²⁻: 3 and 2 both go into 6. Two Al³⁺ give 6+, three S²⁻ give 6− → Al₂S₃.
- **Equal charges:** If the charges are the same size, one of each is enough: Ba²⁺ with S²⁻ → BaS, not Ba₂S₂.
- **Reading a formula:** The small number counts the ion just before it. A missing number means one. The charges are never written in the formula.
- **Naming the compound:** Metal first, then the non-metal with **-ide**: KBr is potassium bromide.

## Section C: Ionic Compounds

### Key facts

- **The bond:** Ionic bonding is the strong *electrostatic attraction* between oppositely charged ions.
- **Giant lattice:** The ions pack in a huge, regular 3D pattern. Each ion is surrounded by ions of the opposite charge, pulling on it from every side.
- **Melting points:** To melt the lattice, a huge number of strong attractions must be overcome, so ionic compounds melt at high temperatures and are solid at room temperature.
- **Conducting:** A current needs charged particles that can *move*. Solid: the ions are locked in place, so no current. Molten or dissolved in water: the ions are free, so it conducts.
- **No molecules:** An ionic compound is not made of separate molecules. NaCl is a *ratio* (one Na⁺ for every Cl⁻), not one small particle.

## Section D: Covalent Bonding

### Key facts

- **Sharing:** Two non-metal atoms each put one electron into a **shared pair**. One shared pair is one covalent bond. The pair counts towards the outer shell of *both* atoms.
- **How many bonds?:** An atom makes as many bonds as the electrons it is short of a full shell. Hydrogen (1 short) makes 1. Carbon (2,4: 4 short) makes 4.
- **Double bonds:** Two shared pairs between the same two atoms make a **double bond**: 4 shared electrons. An atom that is 2 short can fill its shell this way.
- **Dot-and-cross:** Draw one atom’s electrons as dots and the other’s as crosses. Each shared pair sits where the two outer shells overlap.
- **Simple molecules:** The bonds *inside* a molecule are strong, but the forces *between* molecules are weak. Little energy separates them: low melting and boiling points.
- **No current:** Molecules have no overall charge and no free electrons, so covalent substances do not conduct, even when melted.

## Section E: Metallic Bonding & Telling Them Apart

### Key facts

- **Metallic bonding:** Metal atoms give up their outer electrons to the whole structure. Positive metal ions sit in layers in a “sea” of **delocalised** electrons, and the two attract.
- **Conducting:** The delocalised electrons drift through the metal when a voltage is applied, so metals conduct as solids and when melted.
- **Bending:** Layers of ions can slide over each other while the sea of electrons keeps holding them together, so metals bend and can be hammered into shape.
- **Spot the type:** metal + non-metal → ionic · non-metal + non-metal → covalent · metal(s) only → metallic
