Section A · Q1–Q8 · Human Biology

The Excretory System

Video: how kidneys work →
Key facts

Excretion

Removing the waste made by chemical reactions inside the body’s cells. The lungs excrete carbon dioxide, the skin loses some in sweat, and the kidneys excrete urea in urine.

Urea

When the body has more amino acids (from protein) than it needs, the liver breaks them down into urea. The blood carries urea to the kidneys. If it builds up, it is poisonous.

The urinary system

Two kidneys filter the blood. Each sends urine down a ureter to the bladder, which stores it. Urine leaves the body through the urethra.

Blood in, blood out

The renal artery brings blood to each kidney. The renal vein takes the cleaned blood away.

How a kidney filters

About a million tiny tubes called nephrons filter the blood. Useful things (all the glucose, most of the water, the salts the body needs) are taken back into the blood. Urea, extra water and extra salt stay behind as urine.

Water balance

The kidneys keep the water in the blood steady. They take back more water when the body is short of it, and less when there is plenty.

Section B · Q9–Q16 · Plant Biology

Absorption, Transport & Transpiration

Plant Biology unit test → Video →
Key facts

Root hair cells

Each has a long, thin outgrowth that pokes between soil particles. Millions of them give the root a huge surface area for taking in water (by osmosis) and mineral ions.

Xylem

Long, hollow tubes of dead cells with strong walls. They carry water and minerals from the roots, up the stem, to the leaves. Water in xylem only goes up. The walls also help hold the plant up.

Phloem

Tubes of living cells that carry dissolved sugar made in the leaves to every part that needs it: growing tips, roots, fruits and stores. Phloem carries sap both up and down.

Transpiration

Water evaporates inside the leaf and escapes as water vapour through tiny pores called stomata. As water leaves the top, more is pulled up the xylem behind it, like drinking through a straw.

Stomata

Each stoma is opened and closed by two guard cells. Open stomata let carbon dioxide in for photosynthesis, but water vapour gets out the same way. Most close in the dark.

What speeds it up

Transpiration is faster when it is hotter, windier, drier (low humidity) and brighter.

Section C · Q17–Q24 · Plant Biology

The Carbon Cycle

Plant Biology unit test → Video →
Key facts

Where carbon is stored

In the air as carbon dioxide; in living things as glucose, starch, fats and proteins; in dead matter; and in fossil fuels (coal, oil and gas) and limestone rock.

Photosynthesis

Plants take carbon dioxide from the air and build it into glucose:
carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen

Respiration

Plants, animals and microorganisms all respire, all the time, and give carbon dioxide back to the air:
glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water

Feeding

When an animal eats a plant or another animal, the carbon compounds pass along the food chain.

Decomposers

Bacteria and fungi feed on dead organisms and waste. As they respire, the carbon goes back into the air as carbon dioxide.

Fossil fuels and combustion

Living things buried millions of years ago, where decomposers could not reach them, slowly became coal, oil and gas. Combustion (burning) releases their carbon as carbon dioxide in a few seconds.

Section D · Q25–Q30 · Thermal Energy

Conservation of Energy

Sound & thermal energy sheet →
Key facts

The law

Energy is never created or destroyed. It is only transferred from one store to another, or spread out into the surroundings.

Stores

Energy can be stored as chemical (food, fuel, batteries), kinetic (moving things), gravitational potential (raised things), elastic (stretched springs) and thermal (hot things).

Useful and wasted

Every device transfers some energy usefully and some in ways we do not want. energy in = useful energy out + wasted energy out

Where the waste goes

Wasted energy usually ends up heating the surroundings a little. It is spread so thinly that it is very hard to use again.

Sankey diagrams

An arrow diagram where the width of each arrow shows the amount of energy. The input is on the left, useful energy goes straight on, and wasted energy bends away.

Conserving heat (3.3)

Insulation, double glazing and curtains slow the heat lost from a building, so the heating does less work. That saves money and cuts greenhouse gases.

Section E · Q31–Q37 · Chemical Structures

Simple & Giant Structures

Bonding & Ions drill → Video →
Key facts

Simple molecules

Small groups of atoms such as H₂O, CO₂, O₂ and CH₄. The covalent bonds inside each molecule are strong, but the forces between molecules are weak, so they melt and boil at low temperatures.

Giant covalent structures

Huge networks in which every atom is joined to its neighbours by covalent bonds. There are no separate molecules. Melting means breaking millions of strong bonds, so the melting point is very high.

Diamond

Carbon. Every atom is bonded to four others in a rigid 3D network. Extremely hard. No free electrons, so it does not conduct.

Graphite

Carbon. Every atom is bonded to three others in flat sheets. The forces between the sheets are weak. The fourth outer electron of each atom is free to move along the sheets.

Silicon dioxide

The main compound in sand and quartz. A giant covalent structure like diamond, so it is hard and melts at about 1700 °C.

Other giant structures

Ionic compounds (giant ionic lattices) and metals (giant metallic lattices) are giant structures too. Only simple molecules have low melting points.
