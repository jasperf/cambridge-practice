## Section A: Electricity: Meters, Symbols & Resistance

**Q1** (1 mark): Ammeter. It is in series, in the main loop, so it measures the current.

**Q2** (1 mark): Voltmeter. It is connected across R to measure the voltage across it.

**Q3** (1 mark): **C**: In parallel, on its own branch joined either side of R. Y is on a separate branch that joins the circuit on each side of R, so it is in parallel with R. That is how a voltmeter must be connected. A and D describe series connections, which is how an ammeter goes.

**Q4** (1 mark): Variable resistor: a resistor (rectangle) with an arrow through it, so its resistance can be changed.

**Q5** (1 mark): Lamp (bulb): a circle with a cross inside.

**Q6** (1 mark): Switch (shown open, so no current flows).

**Q7** (1 mark): About 48 Ω (accept 45–51). The curve at 5 resistors is between 60 Ω (4 resistors) and 40 Ω (6 resistors); 240 ÷ 5 = 48 Ω.

**Q8** (1 mark): **A**: The total resistance falls, but by a smaller amount with each resistor added. It falls from 240 to 120 Ω (a drop of 120), then 120 to 80 (a drop of 40), then 80 to 60 (a drop of 20). It keeps going down, but each drop is smaller, so the graph is a curve that levels out, not a straight line.

**Q9** (2 marks): **D**: Turning the variable resistor to a higher resistance makes the current smaller, so the lamp gets dimmer. In series, the same current flows through the variable resistor and the lamp. More resistance means less current, so the lamp is dimmer. A fixed resistor cannot be changed (that is the point of a variable one), and the battery's voltage does not change.

## Section E: Thermal Energy & Investigations

**Q34** (1 mark): **D**: They are at the same temperature, but the pool has far more thermal energy because it has many more particles. The particles in both move at the same average speed, so the temperature is the same. But the pool has vastly more particles, so the total energy (thermal energy) is much bigger. Temperature is an average; thermal energy is a total.

**Q35** (1 mark): **C**: Its particles move faster and spread out, so the warm water becomes less dense than the cooler water above it. Heated particles move faster and spread further apart, so the same mass of water takes up more space: it becomes less dense and rises. The cooler, denser water sinks to take its place. The particles themselves do not get bigger (B), and it is the water that moves, not heat on its own (A).

**Q36** (1 mark): A convection current.

**Q37** (1 mark): **A**: Yes, because the three repeat results are close together. The results only spread over 4 000 J (168 000 to 172 000), so they are close together: precise. Precise does not tell you whether they are near the true value (that is accuracy, B), and repeats never need to be identical (C).

**Q38** (1 mark): (168 000 + 172 000 + 170 000) ÷ 3 = 510 000 ÷ 3 = 170 000 J.

**Q39** (2 marks): **B**: Put a lid on the beaker and wrap it in insulation, so less energy escapes to the surroundings. Insulating the beaker (and using a lid, or a polystyrene cup instead of metal) cuts the energy lost to the surroundings, so the reading gets closer to the true value. Repeating (A) makes results more reliable but does not remove an error that happens every time.
