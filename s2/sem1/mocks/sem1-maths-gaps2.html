Words to Expressions in n

Expressions & formulae sheet →
Key facts

Do the steps in order

Each then works on the whole result so far. “Subtract 1, then double” is 2(n − 1), not 2n − 1.

Brackets

If a step applies to a sum or difference, that part goes in brackets. Without them, only the last term gets the step.

“Squares” is a power

Squares means “to the power 2” and cubes means “to the power 3”. A square root uses the root sign. Different words, different operations.

Rule from a table

The value at 0 is the fixed charge. The jump from one column to the next is the amount for each extra unit. So cost = fixed + jump × units.

Check with a number

Put a small number in for n, follow the words step by step, and see if the expression gives the same answer.

Simplifying Surds

Rational & irrational sheet →
Key facts

The splitting rule

√(a × b) = √a × √b.

Use the largest square factor

Look for the biggest of 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100 that divides the number. Its root comes out as a whole number in front.

Check by squaring

Square the number in front, then multiply by the number under the root. You should get the number you started with.

Adding surds

Surds add like terms only when the part under the root matches: 3√2 + 4√2 = 7√2. Simplify each one first.

When it stays

If no square number (except 1) divides it, the root is already in its simplest form.

Section C · Q12–Q16 · Rational & Irrational Numbers

Surds in Brackets

Rational & irrational sheet →
Key facts

Expand as usual

Multiply every term in the first bracket by every term in the second. Remember √n × √n = n.

Difference of two squares

(a + b)(a − b) = a² − b². The two middle terms are opposites, so they cancel.

Why it matters

When the middle terms cancel, the surds vanish. The answer can be a whole number even though the brackets hold a surd.

No calculator

Leave answers as surds or whole numbers. Never turn √n into a rounded decimal.

📐 Do the surds really disappear?

Slide n. The product (√n + 3)(√n − 3) is always n − 9, even when √n is not a whole number.

Powers, Zero, Negative & Powers of 10

Indices & standard form sheet →
Key facts

Use the table

Powers of the same base: multiply means add the powers (72 × 74 = 76), divide means subtract. A table of powers lets you read the answer off.

Zero power

Any number (except 0) to the power 0 is 1.

Negative power

x−n = 1 ÷ xn. It is a fraction, not a negative number, and x−1 is not x.

Multiplying by 10n

Multiplying by 103 makes a number 1000 times bigger. Multiplying by 10−3 is the same as dividing by 1000.

Dividing by 10−n

Dividing by 10−n is the same as multiplying by 10n. Two minuses make it bigger.

Indices & Fractions

Indices & standard form sheet →
Key facts

Three laws

Multiply: add the powers. Divide: subtract the powers. Power of a power: multiply the powers.

Numbers and letters separately

Work out the numbers first, then deal with each letter by itself.

Cube or square the whole bracket

In (3m2)4 the power outside applies to the 3 as well as to the m2.

A negative power in a fraction

An answer such as y−5 is written as 1/y5 when the question wants a fraction.

Brackets, Signs & Equations

Simultaneous equations sheet →
Key facts

Two brackets

Multiply every term by every term, then collect the like terms in the middle.

Signs

A negative times a negative is positive. A minus in front of a bracket changes every sign inside it.

Subtracting equations

Put the second equation in brackets first. The minus then applies to every term, including the right-hand side.

Then substitute back

After finding one letter, put it into an original equation to find the other, then check it in the other equation.

Convert first

Change mixed numbers to improper fractions before you square, multiply or divide. Never square the whole part and the fraction part separately.

Rational & Irrational Numbers

Rational & irrational sheet →
Key facts

The pizza picture

A rational number is a pizza you can cut fairly: 3 slices out of 4 is 3/4. An irrational number can never be cut fairly, however small the slices. There is always a crumb left over. π is that impossible pizza.

Rational

Can be written as a fraction of two integers (the bottom is not 0). Whole numbers, terminating decimals like 0.25 and recurring decimals like 0.2525… are all rational.

Irrational

Cannot be written as a fraction. The decimal never ends and never repeats. Examples: π, √2, √8.

Roots that fool you

√49 = 7 and ∛27 = 3 are whole numbers, so they are rational. √8 and ∛10 are not perfect roots, so they are irrational.

The Venn diagram

Integers sit inside the rational numbers (every integer is a fraction over 1). Irrational numbers sit outside the rational circle altogether.
