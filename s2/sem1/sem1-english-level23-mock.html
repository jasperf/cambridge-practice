Section B · Q11–Q23

Reading: Text 1

Read Text 1 carefully. Paragraph numbers are in brackets. Answer like a P.E.E.E. writer: Point, Evidence, Explain, Explore.
Text 1 · Student magazine article

Dig In: Why Every School Needs a Garden

[1]Picture this: a strip of bare, cracked concrete behind the sports hall, where crisp packets drift like tumbleweed. Now picture the same space bursting with tomatoes, beans and sunflowers taller than you. Which one would you rather learn beside?

[2]A school garden is not a luxury; it is a classroom without walls. In a recent survey of 1,200 secondary students, 68% said they understood science topics better after hands-on work outdoors. Dr Amara Osei, a child psychologist, explains that “time spent growing things lowers stress and sharpens concentration.” Gardens teach patience, teamwork and responsibility.

[3]Some people argue that gardens cost too much. It is true that tools, soil and seeds are not free. However, most of the cost is paid only once, and a well-run garden can sell what it grows; last year, a school near ours raised enough from chilli sales to buy a small greenhouse.

[4]Last spring, my class planted a single row of spinach. Most of us had never touched soil on purpose. By June, Rafi, who usually hid at the back of every lesson, was the one explaining to visitors how the watering system worked. The garden gave him something no textbook had: a reason to care.

[5]We spend our days staring at screens. We spend our breaks staring at screens. Isn’t it time we spent one hour a week staring at something that is actually alive? Talk to your student council. Sign the petition outside the library. Let’s dig in, together.

Section C · Q24–Q31

Reading: Text 2 & Comparing

Text 2 is a reply to Text 1. Questions 24–28 are about Text 2 on its own; 29–31 compare the two texts.
Text 2 · Letter to the editor

Gardens Can Wait

[1]I read last week’s article about school gardens with interest, and I agree that fresh air is good for young people. But I believe our school has more urgent needs.

[2]Firstly, our science laboratory still has only six working microscopes for a class of thirty. Students share textbooks with loose pages and missing covers. Before we spend money on soil and seeds, we should fix the tools that every student uses every day.

[3]Secondly, a garden needs care during the long holidays, when nobody is here to water it. Without a clear plan, the beautiful garden in the article would be a patch of dead stalks by the time term starts again.

[4]I am not against gardens. I am against doing things in the wrong order. Let us repair the classrooms first; the garden can come next year.

A parent
