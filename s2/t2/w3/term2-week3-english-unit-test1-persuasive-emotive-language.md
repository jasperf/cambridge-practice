## Part A: Persuasive Techniques — Level 2/3

**Key ideas:** **Identify it · Quote it · Explain its effect.** To analyse a technique, name the **technique**, quote the **evidence**, say the **purpose** (why the writer used it) and explain the **effect** on the reader. Ms. Atiya’s posters list twelve techniques; use the cards below as a checklist, then apply them to new sentences.

| Technique | What it is |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Rhetorical question** | A question asked for effect, not for an answer. |
| **Emotive language** | Words chosen to stir up feelings. |
| **Direct address** | Speaking straight to the reader as “you”. |
| **Repetition** | Coming back to a key word or idea. |
| **Facts & statistics** | Information and numbers that can be checked. |
| **Rule of three** | Three related words or ideas in a row. |
| **Expert opinion** | The view of a qualified person. |
| **Anecdote / example** | A short, real or relatable case. |
| **Inclusive language** | Words like “we”, “us” and “our”. |
| **Imperative verbs** | Command words that tell the reader to act. |
| **Concession** | Admitting a point from the other side first. |
| **Juxtaposition** | Contrasting things placed side by side. |

## Part B: Emotive Language — Level 2/3

**Key ideas:** **Emotive language** is made up of words chosen mainly for the feelings they stir up rather than for the facts they carry. A word’s **connotation** is the feeling or idea attached to it beyond its dictionary meaning. When you explain the effect of emotive language, name the **feeling** created in the reader (for example sympathy, guilt, anger, fear or urgency) and link it to the writer’s **purpose**.

> **Extract 2 — Dog shelter appeal (leaflet)**
>
> Every night, hundreds of frightened dogs shiver in cold concrete cages, begging for a family. These loyal, innocent animals have done nothing wrong, yet they are abandoned and forgotten. Their desperate eyes follow every visitor. Will you turn your back on them, or will you finally give them the home they deserve?

## Part C: Grammar & Vocabulary — Level 1

**Key ideas:** A **simple** sentence has one clause; a **compound** sentence joins two main clauses with *for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*; a **complex** sentence has a main clause and a subordinate clause that begins with a word like *because, although, when* or *if*. **Conjunctive adverbs** such as *however, therefore, meanwhile* and *eventually* link ideas and usually take a comma after them.

> **Extract 3 — from a short story, “The Last Bus”**
>
> The last bus of the evening wheezed to a stop, and Mara climbed aboard with her heart thudding. In her coat pocket, the scholarship letter had gone soft from being unfolded and refolded all day. She had not shown it to anyone. If she told her parents, she would have to explain the cost; if she kept it secret, she would have to explain nothing — and that seemed easier. The driver, an old man with a grey moustache, glanced at her in the mirror. “Long day?” he asked. Mara forced a smile and stared out of the window at the streetlights smearing past, orange and blurred. Somewhere between the third stop and the fourth, she made up her mind.

## Part E: Short Story Writing — Level 1

**Key ideas:** A short story usually follows a **plot arc**: exposition (setting and characters), rising action (the problem grows), climax (the turning point), falling action and resolution. Good writers **show, don’t tell**, use sensory detail, and punctuate dialogue correctly: a new line for each new speaker, speech marks around the spoken words, and the final punctuation inside the closing speech mark.
