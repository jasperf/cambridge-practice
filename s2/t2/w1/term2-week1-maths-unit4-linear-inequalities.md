## Section A: Symbols & Words

**Why should I care?** An equation says two things are **exactly equal**. Real life is usually looser: a budget is a limit, a target is a minimum.
- **Phone plans:** $20 a month plus $0.50 per GB, with only $35 to spend. You do not need to use *exactly* 30 GB. You need to use **at most** 30 GB.
- **Lifts and bridges:** a lift can carry **at most** 800 kg; a bus must be **less than** 3.5 m tall to fit under a bridge.
- **Targets:** collect **at least** 160 blankets. Collecting 170 is fine.
- **Later on:** the same idea is how a shop decides how many items to sell to make a profit, and how engineers set safe limits.

An **inequality** compares two things that are not necessarily equal. There are four symbols:

| Symbol | Read as | Number line circle |
| --- | --- | --- |
| x > 3 | *x* is **greater than** 3 | **open** circle at 3 (3 itself is not allowed) |
| x < 3 | *x* is **less than** 3 | **open** circle at 3 |
| x ≥ 3 | *x* is **greater than or equal to** 3 | **closed** circle at 3 (3 is allowed) |
| x ≤ 3 | *x* is **less than or equal to** 3 | **closed** circle at 3 |

**Words to symbols (learn these):**
- **at least** means ≥ (the smallest it can be, or more)
- **at most** means ≤ (the largest it can be, or less)
- **more than** or **greater than** means >
- **less than** or **fewer than** means <

The pointed end of the symbol always faces the **smaller** side: in x < 3, *x* is the smaller one.

## Section B: Number Lines

**Reading a number line:** the circle sits on the boundary number, and the arrow shows which way the allowed values go.
- **Closed (filled) circle** = the boundary number **is** included (≤ or ≥)
- **Open (empty) circle** = the boundary number is **not** included (< or >)
- Arrow to the **right** = bigger numbers (> or ≥); arrow to the **left** = smaller numbers (< or ≤)

The class example x ≤ −20 is drawn with a closed circle at −20 and an arrow pointing left, towards −21, −22 and so on.

## Section C: Solving Linear Inequalities

**Solve it like an equation.** Treat ≤, ≥, <, > as if they were =, and do the same thing to both sides. The class example:

1. **Write it:** 3x + 4 ≤ 2x − 16
2. **Collect the x terms on one side and the numbers on the other:** 3x − 2x ≤ −16 − 4
3. **Simplify:** x ≤ −20
4. **Draw it:** a closed circle at −20 with an arrow pointing left.

**Check by testing a number:** pick one allowed value, such as −25. Left side: 3 × (−25) + 4 = −71. Right side: 2 × (−25) − 16 = −66. Is −71 ≤ −66? Yes, so the answer works.

**The one new rule: flip the sign.** If you **multiply or divide both sides by a negative number**, the inequality sign turns round. Check with numbers: 2 < 5 is true, but multiply both sides by −1 and you get −2 and −5, and −2 > −5. So the sign must flip.

−2x < 10 becomes x > −5 after dividing both sides by −2. **Adding or subtracting** a number never flips the sign, whatever its sign.

## Section D: Word Problems

**Four steps for a word problem:**
1. **Let** a letter stand for what you need to find, for example *x* = the number of gigabytes.
2. **Spot the key words** (at least, at most, more than, fewer than) and choose the symbol.
3. **Write the inequality** and solve it as before.
4. **Read the answer back in words.** If you can only have whole things (people, boxes, buses), a decimal answer must be rounded in the **safe** direction.

**Class example 1:** a phone company charges $20 a month plus $0.50 per GB. The budget is $35. Then 20 + 0.5x ≤ 35, so 0.5x ≤ 15, so x ≤ 30. The most you can use is 30 GB.

**Class example 2:** a club wants **at least** 160 blankets and already has 42 + 65. Then 42 + 65 + x ≥ 160, so 107 + x ≥ 160, so x ≥ 53. They must collect at least 53 more.
