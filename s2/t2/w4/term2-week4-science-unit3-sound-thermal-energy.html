Section A

Longitudinal and Transverse Waves

A wave transfers energy from a source to a receiver without transferring matter. In a transverse wave the particles vibrate at right angles to the direction the wave travels (light, water ripples): the highest part is a crest and the lowest part a trough. In a longitudinal wave the particles vibrate parallel to the direction of travel, making compressions (closer together) and rarefactions (further apart). Sound is a longitudinal wave.

Section B

Amplitude, Wavelength, Frequency and Period

Amplitude is the height of the wave from the middle line to a crest (or trough). Wavelength is the distance between two consecutive crests (or two compressions). Frequency is the number of waves passing a point each second, measured in hertz (Hz). The period is the time for one complete wave to pass a point, so T = 1 ÷ f.

Section C

Loudness, Pitch and Interference

A larger amplitude makes a louder sound; a higher frequency makes a higher pitch. When two waves overlap in the same place they combine — this is superposition. Waves that reinforce each other show constructive interference (crest meets crest, trough meets trough). Waves that cancel show destructive interference (crest meets trough).

Section D

Heat and Temperature (3.2)

Thermal energy is produced when atoms and molecules move faster and collide more. Heat is the transfer of thermal energy between objects because of a temperature difference, measured in joules (J). Temperature measures how hot or cold an object is — the average kinetic energy of its particles — and is measured in degrees Celsius (°C). It decides which way heat flows: from high temperature to low.

Same Temperature, Different Heat

Section E

Heat Transfer and Conserving Heat (3.3)

Conduction: heat passes through direct contact between particles, mainly in solids (metals are best). Convection: heat is carried by the movement of a liquid or gas — warmer, less dense fluid rises and cooler, denser fluid sinks. Radiation: heat travels as infrared waves, even through a vacuum. Heat can be conserved with insulation (loft, clothing layers) and double-glazed windows, which saves energy, cuts costs and reduces emissions.

Section F

Cooling by Evaporation (3.6)

Evaporation happens at the surface of a liquid and can happen at any temperature. Boiling happens at the boiling point and bubbles of vapour form throughout the liquid. Evaporation cools a liquid because the fastest, most energetic particles are the ones that escape.
