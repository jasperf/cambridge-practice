## Section A: The Sentence & Simple Sentences

### Key Concepts
- **Every sentence** requires two essential parts:
  - **Subject** — who/what is being discussed
  - **Predicate** — the part containing the verb

- **Simple sentence** contains only one independent clause (one subject + one predicate), but either can be *compound* (more than one)

### Simple Sentence Types
| Abbreviation | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **SS** | Single Subject / Single Predicate | "Dogs bark." |
| **CS** | Compound Subject / Single Predicate | "Dogs and cats sleep." |
| **SC** | Single Subject / Compound Predicate | "Dogs bark and howl." |
| **CC** | Compound Subject / Compound Predicate | "Dogs and cats bark and sleep." |

- **Compound subject** = subject containing more than one person, place, or thing
- **Compound predicate** = predicate with more than one verb/action

## Section B: Compound Sentences — FANBOYS

### Key Concepts
- **Compound sentence** = joins **two or more independent clauses**
- **FANBOYS** = coordinating conjunctions: **F**or, **A**nd, **N**or, **B**ut, **O**r, **Y**et, **S**o

### FANBOYS Meanings & Usage
| Word | Meaning | Punctuation |
|---|---|---|
| **for** | gives a reason (similar to "because") | comma before |
| **and** | adds information | comma before (optional if clauses are short) |
| **nor** | also not — requires **inverted word order** | comma before |
| **but** | contrasts two ideas | comma before |
| **or** | shows two equally weighted options | comma before |
| **yet** | contrasts two ideas (similar to "but") | comma before |
| **so** | shows result (similar to "therefore") | comma before |

- **Comma rule**: Usually place a comma **before** the FANBOYS conjunction. Exception: comma is optional when **both independent clauses are short**.

## Section C: Compound Sentences — Conjunctive Adverbs (MOTH) & Semicolons

### Key Concepts
- **Conjunctive adverbs** = adverbs that connect independent clauses
- **MOTH** = memory acronym: **M**oreover, **O**therwise, **T**herefore, **H**owever

### Punctuation Rules
- **Before conjunctive adverb**: **semicolon**
- **After conjunctive adverb**: **comma**
- **Semicolon-only rule**: Can join two closely related independent clauses with **only a semicolon** (no conjunction/adverb)

### Conjunctive Adverb Categories
| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Contrast** | however, nevertheless, nonetheless |
| **Cause-Effect** | therefore, consequently, thus, hence |
| **Comparison** | likewise, similarly |
| **Alternative** | otherwise, alternatively |
| **Time/Order** | meanwhile |

## Section D: Complex & Compound-Complex Sentences

### Key Concepts
- **Independent clause** = complete thought that can stand alone as a sentence
- **Dependent clause** = incomplete thought; cannot stand alone; begins with a **dependent marker**

### Dependent Markers (Subordinating Conjunctions)
Common examples: although, because, if, when, since, unless, until, after, while, as

### Complex Sentences
- Contains **at least one independent clause + at least one dependent clause**
- **Comma rule**:
  - **Dependent clause first**: comma **after** the dependent clause
  - **Dependent clause second**: **no comma**

### Compound-Complex Sentences
- Contains **at least two independent clauses + one or more dependent clauses**

## Section E: Reading Comprehension with P.E.E.E

### P.E.E.E Framework
A structured method for answering comprehension questions:

1. **P**oint — Your main idea that **directly answers the question**
2. **E**vidence — A **short, well-chosen quotation** from the text that supports your point
3. **E**xplanation — **Interpret** how the evidence supports your point; **do not simply repeat** the quotation
4. **E**xplore — **Zoom in** on one word, phrase, or technique from the quotation; **analyse its effect on the reader**

### Tips for Strong P.E.E.E Answers
- Choose **short, precise** quotations for Evidence (1-2 words or a brief phrase)
- Explanation must **interpret** — explain what the quotation reveals, not restate it
- Explore should analyse **literary techniques** (metaphor, simile, personification, alliteration, etc.) and their impact
- Avoid **long, vague** quotations — they rarely support your point effectively

### Common Literary Techniques
| Technique | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Metaphor** | "an invisible, inevitable sword of heat" | Creates vivid imagery; suggests unstoppable power |
| **Simile** | "like a knife" | Compares two things using "like" or "as" |
| **Personification** | "Death leaped from man to man" | Gives human qualities to non-human things |
| **Alliteration** | "bigger and bigger" | Repetition of consonant sounds for emphasis |
| **Repetition** | Repeating a word for effect | Emphasises the word's importance |

## Quick Reference: Sentence Types

| Type | Clauses | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | 1 independent | Subject + Predicate | "She runs." |
| **Compound** | 2+ independent | FANBOYS or semicolon | "She runs, and he walks." |
| **Complex** | 1 independent + 1+ dependent | Dependent marker | "Because she runs, she wins." / "She wins because she runs." |
| **Compound-Complex** | 2+ independent + 1+ dependent | Combination | "Because she runs, she wins, and he loses." |

## Punctuation Cheat Sheet

| Situation | Punctuation |
|---|---|
| FANBOYS conjunction | Comma before (optional for short clauses) |
| Conjunctive adverb (MOTH) | Semicolon before, comma after |
| Semicolon-only joining | Semicolon only (clauses must be very closely related) |
| Dependent clause first | Comma after dependent clause |
| Dependent clause second | No comma |
