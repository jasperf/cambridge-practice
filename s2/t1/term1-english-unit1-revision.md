## Section A: The Sentence & Simple Sentences

**Key ideas:** Every sentence needs a **subject** (who/what is being discussed) and a **predicate** (the part containing the verb). A simple sentence contains only one subject and one predicate, but that subject or predicate can be *compound* (more than one person/place/thing, or more than one action). The four simple-sentence types are: **SS** (single subject/single predicate), **CS** (compound subject/single predicate), **SC** (single subject/compound predicate), **CC** (compound subject/compound predicate).

## Section B: Compound Sentences — FANBOYS

**Key ideas:** A compound sentence joins **two or more independent clauses**. The first way to do this is with a **coordinating conjunction** — remember them with **FANBOYS**: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so. A comma usually goes before the conjunction, though it can be dropped if both clauses are short.

## Section C: Compound Sentences — Conjunctive Adverbs (MOTH) & Semicolons

**Key ideas:** A compound sentence can also be formed with a **conjunctive adverb** — remember four of them with **MOTH**: Moreover, Otherwise, Therefore, However. Punctuation rule: **semicolon before**, **comma after** the conjunctive adverb. A third way to join independent clauses is with **only a semicolon**, but only when the two clauses are very closely related.

## Section D: Complex & Compound-Complex Sentences

**Key ideas:** A **complex sentence** has at least one independent clause plus at least one **dependent clause**, joined by a **dependent marker** (e.g. although, because, if, when, since, unless...). Comma rule: comma **after** the dependent clause if it comes first; **no comma** if it comes after the independent clause. A **compound-complex** sentence combines at least two independent clauses with one or more dependent clauses.

## Section E: Reading Comprehension with P.E.E.E

**Key ideas:** P.E.E.E stands for **Point** (your main idea, directly answering the question), **Evidence** (a short, well-chosen quotation from the text), **Explanation** (how the evidence supports your point — don't just repeat the quotation), and **Explore** (zoom in on one word, phrase or technique and analyse its effect on the reader).

**Passage:**
> Sam stood outside the examination room. His notes shook in his hands. Everyone else appeared calm, but his thoughts crashed into one another. When his name was called, he took a slow breath and stepped forward.

**Question used below:** *How does the writer present Sam's challenge?*

## Extended Writing (not auto-marked — for teacher or self/peer review)

There is no single correct answer for these four tasks. Use the checklist at the end to review your own work, or ask your teacher/a study partner to check it.

**EW1 — Compound sentences.** Write a short paragraph (5 sentences) persuading your grandmother to buy a smartphone, using **only compound sentences** joined with FANBOYS conjunctions. Try to vary which conjunction you use.

**EW2 — Complex sentences.** Combine each pair of simple sentences into **one** complex sentence using an appropriate dependent marker. Punctuate correctly.
1. Drones are fun. Not everybody likes them.
2. Young people face a technical problem. They simply Google the answer.
3. Schools fail to teach children digital skills. Technology will end up mastering them.

**EW3 — Reading comprehension, full P.E.E.E.** Read the passage about Leila below, then answer the question **"How does the writer present Leila's challenge?"** using a full P.E.E.E paragraph (Point, Evidence, Explanation, Explore).

> Leila stared at the final step of the climbing wall. Her arms burned, and doubt whispered that she should let go. Below her, the ground seemed impossibly distant. She tightened her grip, reached upward and pulled herself onto the platform.

**EW4 — Mission Possible (sentence variety).** Write a paragraph (10 sentences) persuading your grandmother to buy a smartphone. Use a mix of sentence types: **1 simple, 3 compound, 4 complex, 2 compound-complex.** Label each sentence in brackets with its type, e.g. *(Simple)*, *(Compound)*, *(Complex)*, *(Compound-Complex)*.

### Self-review checklist

- [ ] EW1: Are all 5 sentences compound, each with a comma + FANBOYS conjunction before the second clause?
- [ ] EW2: Does each new sentence have a dependent marker and correct comma placement?
- [ ] EW3: Point directly answers the question; Evidence is a short, accurate quotation; Explanation interprets (not repeats) the quotation; Explore zooms into one word/technique and explains its effect on the reader.
- [ ] EW4: Does the paragraph contain exactly 1 simple, 3 compound, 4 complex and 2 compound-complex sentences, correctly labelled and punctuated?
