Section A · Level 2/3

Viewpoint & Argument

In everyday life an argument is a disagreement. In academic work it is something calmer: a clear claim supported by reasons and evidence — a structured exploration of an idea, not an attempt to “win”. A viewpoint is the writer’s overall belief about the topic; the argument is the case the writer builds to make that belief convincing. Always ask two more questions of any persuasive text: purpose (what is it trying to do?) and audience (who needs to be convinced?).

Section B · Level 2/3

Parts of an Argument

The four core elements of a written argument, in the order they normally appear in a paragraph. Learn the question each one answers — that is how you identify them in a text.

1. Claim

What is the writer saying?

The specific statement the writer wants us to accept. It must be arguable, not just a statement of fact.

2. Reason

Why does the writer think this?

Logical support for the claim. It follows the word because: if the claim is challenged with “Why do you say that?”, the reason is your answer.

3. Evidence

What support can the writer provide?

A fact, example, statistic, research finding or expert opinion that proves the reason is valid.

4. Explanation

How does this evidence prove the point?

The logical bridge: it shows how the evidence supports the reason and the claim, and tells the reader why that information matters.

How the four parts fit together
The viewpoint is the belief underneath. The four parts are the case built on top of it.

Section C · Level 2/3

Word Choices

Persuasive writing lives or dies on word choice. A hook opens the introduction; a thesis statement states your opinion and what should be done; transition words connect one argument to the next; specific examples beat general ones every time. Avoid sweeping general statements — everybody knows…, nobody wants…, anyone can see… — because nothing about them can be proved.
Q19
Why should you avoid writing Everybody knows that… in a persuasive essay?
1 mark
It is a little informal, though the point it makes is still proved
It uses the wrong tense for an essay
It gives away the writer’s audience too early
It is a sweeping general statement — it cannot be proved, so it does not work as evidence

Section D · Level 2/3

Text Features

Text features are the structural signposts a persuasive text uses to guide its reader: the introduction with its hook and thesis, subheadings, topic sentences opening each body paragraph, supporting details and statistics, an acknowledged counter-argument, and a conclusion that paraphrases the thesis. Each one has a job — in the test you may be asked what that job is.

The sentence at the beginning of a body paragraph that clearly states its idea

Any sentence in the text that contains a statistic

What job does a subheading do in a persuasive text?

Section E · Level 1

Past Continuous Tense

The past continuous is formed with was / were + the -ing form of the verb. Use was with I, he, she, it and were with you, we, they. It describes an action that was already in progress at a moment in the past — often one that a shorter past-simple action interrupted (when), or two longer actions running side by side (while). The negative is wasn’t / weren’t + -ing.
Past continuous vs past simple — the interruption
The long bar is the past continuous. The single point on it is the past simple.

Section F · English Support

Subject–Verb Agreement

A singular subject takes a singular verb; a plural subject takes a plural verb. Four traps to watch for: (1) a phrase between the subject and the verb (the list of reasons is…) — ignore it and find the real subject; (2) words like everyone, each, nobody, somebody are always singular; (3) with there is / there are, the verb agrees with what comes after it; (4) with either… or and neither… nor, the verb agrees with the subject nearest to it.

The evidence supports the writer’s reason.
