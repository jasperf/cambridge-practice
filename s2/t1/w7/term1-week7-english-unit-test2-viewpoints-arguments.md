## Part A: Viewpoint & Argument

**Key ideas:** In everyday life an **argument** is a disagreement. In academic work it is something calmer: a **clear claim supported by reasons and evidence** — a structured exploration of an idea, not an attempt to "win". A **viewpoint** is the writer's overall belief about the topic; the **argument** is the case the writer builds to make that belief convincing. Always ask two more questions of a persuasive text: **purpose** (what is it trying to do?) and **audience** (who needs to be convinced?).

## Part B: Parts of an Argument

**Key ideas:** The four core elements of a written argument, in the order they normally appear in a paragraph. Learn the question each one answers — that is how you identify them in a text.

| Part | Meaning | The question it answers |
|------|---------|-------------------------|
| **Viewpoint** | What the writer generally thinks about the topic | What does the writer believe? |
| **1. Claim** | The specific statement the writer wants us to accept. It must be arguable, not just a statement of fact. | What is the writer saying? |
| **2. Reason** | Logical support for the claim. It follows the word **because**. | Why does the writer think like this? |
| **3. Evidence** | A fact, example, statistic, research finding or expert opinion that proves the reason is valid. | What support can the writer provide? |
| **4. Explanation** | The logical bridge showing how the evidence supports the reason and the claim. | How does this evidence prove the point? |

Evidence provides the supporting information; the explanation tells the reader why that information matters.

## Part C: Word Choices

**Key ideas:** A **hook** opens the introduction; a **thesis statement** states your opinion and what should be done; **transition words** connect one argument to the next; **specific** examples beat general ones every time. Avoid sweeping general statements — *everybody knows…, nobody wants…, anyone can see…* — because nothing about them can be proved.

## Part D: Text Features

**Key ideas:** **Text features** are the structural signposts a persuasive text uses to guide its reader: the introduction with its hook and thesis, subheadings, **topic sentences** opening each body paragraph, supporting details and statistics, an acknowledged **counter-argument**, and a conclusion that paraphrases the thesis. Each one has a job — in the test you may be asked what that job is.

## Part E: Past Continuous Tense — Level 1

**Key ideas:** The past continuous is formed with **was** / **were** + the **-ing** form of the verb. Use *was* with *I, he, she, it* and *were* with *you, we, they*. It describes an action that **was already in progress** at a moment in the past — often one that a shorter past-simple action **interrupted** (*when*), or two longer actions running side by side (*while*). The negative is **wasn't** / **weren't** + -ing.

> *I **was reading** the article* ← the long action already in progress
> *…**when** my sister **called** me* ← the short past-simple action that interrupts it

## Part F: Subject–Verb Agreement — English Support

**Key ideas:** A **singular subject takes a singular verb**; a plural subject takes a plural verb. Four traps to watch for:

1. A phrase between the subject and the verb — *the list ~~of reasons~~ **is**…* — ignore it and find the real subject.
2. Words like *everyone, each, nobody, somebody* are always **singular**.
3. With *there is / there are*, the verb agrees with what comes **after** it.
4. With *either… or* and *neither… nor*, the verb agrees with the subject **nearest** to it.
