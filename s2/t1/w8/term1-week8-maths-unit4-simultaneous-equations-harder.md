## Section A: Break It Down

**Why does 2x + 3y = 13 and 5x − 2y = 4 feel hard?** If you add them you get 7*x* + *y* = 17. If you subtract them you get −3*x* + 5*y* = 9. Either way, **both letters are still there**, so you are stuck. Nothing cancels because no pair of terms is the same size. The fix is to **make** a pair the same size first, by multiplying.

**The five-step recipe** (use it every time):
1. **Line up** the equations: *x* under *x*, *y* under *y*, number under number.
2. **Pick a letter and make its sizes match.** Look at the two numbers in front of it (3 and 2 in front of *y*). Find a number both go into (6). Multiply **every** term of each equation, including the number after the = sign.
3. **Look at the signs** of the matching terms. **S**ame **S**igns **S**ubtract (+6*y* and +6*y*). Different signs add (+6*y* and −6*y*).
4. **Solve** the one-letter equation that is left.
5. **Substitute back** into an original equation to find the other letter, then **check** both values in the other original equation.

**Why are we allowed to multiply?** An equation is a balance. Doubling everything on both sides keeps it balanced: if 2*x* + 3*y* = 13 is true, then 4*x* + 6*y* = 26 is true for the same *x* and *y*. The answer does not change; only how the equation looks.

**The worked example, step by step** (the interactive sheet animates this in its Elimination Animator):

```text
   2x + 3y = 13      × 2
   5x − 2y =  4      × 3

   4x + 6y = 26      (13 × 2 = 26)
+ 15x − 6y = 12      (4 × 3 = 12)
  -------------
  19x + 0y = 38      +6y and −6y: different signs, so ADD

  x = 38 ÷ 19 = 2
  2(2) + 3y = 13  →  3y = 9  →  y = 3
  check: 5(2) − 2(3) = 10 − 6 = 4 ✓
```

You can also remove *x* instead: multiply by 5 and by 2 to get 10*x* in both, then subtract. The numbers are bigger, but the answer is the same: *x* = 2, *y* = 3. Either letter works; pick the one with the easier numbers.

Now do the same moves yourself, on a new pair: 3x + 4y = 18 and 5x − 3y = 1. You will get rid of *y*.

## Section B: Multiply One Equation

**Look for a number that goes into the other one.** In x + 5y = 17 and 3x + 2y = 12, the *x* numbers are 1 and 3, and 1 goes into 3. So you only need to multiply the **first** equation by 3, to get 3*x* + 15*y* = 51. Leave the second one alone.

**Mistakes to watch for:**
- Multiplying the letters but **not the number after =**.
- Subtracting a negative: 5 − (−2) is 5 + 2 = 7, not 3. Write the brackets in, so you see it.
- Stopping after one letter. The question wants **both** values.

## Section C: Multiply Both Equations

**When neither number goes into the other** (3 and 2, 4 and 5, …), multiply **both** equations. The quick way: multiply each equation by the number in front of the chosen letter in the **other** equation. For 4*x* + 5*y* = … and 3*x* + 2*y* = …, to remove *y* multiply the first by 2 and the second by 5. Both *y* terms become 10*y*.

**Write the signs into the stack.** When you subtract, every column changes sign: 10*y* − (−3*y*) = 13*y* and 8 − (−6) = 14. Writing the brackets stops most sign mistakes.

## Section D: Tidy Up First

**Elimination needs both equations in the same shape**: letters on the left in the order *x*, *y*, and the number on the right. If an equation arrives as 2y = 5x − 3, move the terms across first. Taking 5*x* from both sides gives −5*x* + 2*y* = −3; multiplying everything by −1 turns it into 5*x* − 2*y* = 3, which is easier to read.

**If one equation already says *y* = …**, you do not need elimination at all: put that expression in place of *y* in the other equation (substitution) and use brackets.

## Section E: Why Engineers Need This

**Engineers rarely meet one unknown on its own.** Things in a machine or a circuit affect each other, so each rule an engineer writes down (a law of physics) usually contains **several unknowns at once**. One rule is not enough to find them; you need as many rules as unknowns, and then you solve them *simultaneously*. The three examples below are real methods, made small enough to do by hand:
- **Electrical engineering** — the currents in a circuit with two batteries (Kirchhoff’s laws).
- **Mechanical / civil engineering** — how hard each support of a bridge must push up when a truck drives over it (balance of forces).
- **Computer engineering** — where a laser beam in a game hits a wall (two lines crossing).

**At full size** a chip design or a car frame can have **thousands of unknowns**. Nobody solves those by hand, but the computer runs the same moves you are practising: multiply a row so a term matches, subtract to remove it, repeat. Mathematicians call that recipe *Gaussian elimination*, and it is one of the most-used pieces of code in engineering software. When you learn the recipe for two unknowns, you are learning what that code does.

*On the interactive sheet, each example has an animation that unlocks once its questions are answered: charges flowing round the circuit, a truck driving across the bridge while the support arrows grow and shrink, and a laser beam hitting the wall.*

### ⚡ Electrical: two batteries, two currents

Two loops share the middle wire. Current **I₁** goes round the left loop (14 V battery, 4 Ω resistor) and **I₂** round the right loop (9 V battery, 3 Ω resistor); both flow down through the 2 Ω resistor in the middle, so that resistor carries I₁ + I₂. The rule for each loop is: *battery voltage = resistance × current, added up all the way round*. That gives:
- Left loop: 14 = 4I₁ + 2(I₁ + I₂), which tidies up to 6I₁ + 2I₂ = 14
- Right loop: 9 = 3I₂ + 2(I₁ + I₂), which tidies up to 2I₁ + 5I₂ = 9

Each equation has **both** currents in it, because the middle resistor links the loops.

```text
   +----[ 4 Ω ]----+----[ 3 Ω ]----+
   |               |               |
 14 V   I₁ ↻     [2 Ω]    ↺ I₂    9 V
   |               |               |
   +---------------+---------------+
```

### 🌉 Mechanical: a truck on a bridge

A 10 m bridge rests on two supports, A and B. A 120 kN truck stops 4 m from A. Each support pushes up with an unknown force, R_A and R_B. An engineer uses two balance rules:
- **Up = down** (the bridge does not move up or down): R_A + R_B = 120
- **No turning** (the bridge does not tip). Balancing the turning effects about the middle of the bridge gives: R_A − R_B = 24

Engineers need both forces to choose how strong each support must be.

- B) R_A = 0 kN, R_B = 120 kN

### 💻 Computer: where does the laser hit the wall?

In a game, every straight laser beam and every wall is stored as an equation of a line. To draw the spark in the right place, the game must find the point that is on **both** lines — that is, solve a pair of simultaneous equations. It does this for every beam, every frame, many times a second. The same sum tells a robot vacuum where its path meets the edge of a rug, and a self-driving car where two paths cross.

Laser beam: 3x − y = 4 · Wall: 2x + 3y = 21
