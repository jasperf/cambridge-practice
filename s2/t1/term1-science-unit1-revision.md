## Key Ideas Recap

Read this page before starting. Everything in the test comes from these ideas.

**Subatomic particles**

| Particle | Charge | Where it is |
|----------|--------|-------------|
| Proton | + | Nucleus |
| Neutron | none | Nucleus |
| Electron | − | Shells (energy levels) orbiting the nucleus |

**Atomic number** = number of protons (= number of electrons in a neutral atom).
**Mass number** = protons + neutrons. **Neutrons = mass number − atomic number.**
In the notation ²³₁₁Na, the top number (23) is the mass number and the bottom number (11) is the atomic number.

**Electron shells** fill from the nucleus outwards: **2, 8, 8** for the first three shells (elements 1–20). Sodium (11 electrons) = 2,8,1. Chlorine (17) = 2,8,7.

**Bohr's model** built on Rutherford's by showing electrons move in fixed energy levels (shells), not anywhere at random.

**Periodic table** — elements are ordered by atomic number. **Groups** are the vertical columns: same number of **outer electrons**, similar properties. **Periods** are the horizontal rows: same number of **electron shells**. Metals sit left of the zig-zag line, non-metals to the right. Going **down** a group, atoms get **bigger**.

**Why atoms react** — every atom "wants" a full outer shell: greater stability, lower energy. **Group 8/18** (the noble gases) already have one, so they don't react.

**The three types of bonding**

| Bond type | Electrons | Between | Example |
|-----------|-----------|---------|---------|
| **Ionic** | **Transferred** (lost and gained) | Metal + non-metal | NaCl, MgO, MgCl₂ |
| **Covalent** | **Shared** in pairs | Two non-metals | F₂, H₂, H₂O, SO₂ |
| **Metallic** | **Free** to move — a "sea of electrons" | Metal atoms only | Copper, iron, aluminium |

**Forming ions** — Groups 1–3 **lose** electrons (number lost = group number) → **positive** ions: Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺. Groups 6–7 **gain** electrons (number gained = 8 − group number) → **negative** ions: O²⁻, Cl⁻.

**Ionic formulae** — the total positive charge must balance the total negative charge: Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → NaCl; Mg²⁺ + 2Cl⁻ → MgCl₂; 2Li⁺ + O²⁻ → Li₂O.

**Reactivity trends** — **Group 1**: reactivity **increases** down the group (outer electron further from the nucleus, held less strongly, easier to lose). **Group 7**: reactivity **decreases** down the group (the electron being gained lands closer to the nucleus in small atoms, so it is pulled in more strongly).

## Section B: Electronic Structure

**Key ideas:** Electrons orbit the nucleus in *shells*, also called *energy levels*. The first shell holds up to 2 electrons, the second up to 8, the third up to 8 (for elements 1–20). **Electronic structure** shows how electrons are arranged, e.g. carbon (6 electrons) = 2,4. In 1913 Niels Bohr built on Rutherford's model, showing that electrons move in different energy levels around the nucleus rather than anywhere at random.

**Key ideas:** The periodic table organises all known elements by *atomic number*. **Groups** are vertical columns (elements with similar properties). **Periods** are horizontal rows (elements with the same number of electron shells). **Metals** are on the left and centre, **non-metals** on the right.

## Section D: Metals, Non-metals & Trends

**Key ideas:** **Metals** (left of the zig-zag line) are shiny, malleable, ductile, conduct heat and electricity, and have high melting points. **Non-metals** (right of the zig-zag line) are dull, brittle, poor conductors, and have lower melting points. Going *down* a group, atomic size increases.

# Part 2 · Why Elements React & Ionic Bonding

*Chapter 2.3 — 22 questions, 26 marks*

## Section E: Why Atoms React

**Key ideas:** The outermost shell is the *highest energy level*. An atom has no overall charge because it has equal numbers of protons and electrons. Elements in **Group 8** (the noble gases — also labelled **Group 18** on modern periodic tables) already have a full outermost shell, so they are stable and do not react. All other elements react to try to get a full outer shell too — either by **losing or gaining electrons** (ionic bonding) or by **sharing electrons** (covalent bonding).

## Section F: Forming Ions

**Key ideas:** Metal atoms (Groups 1 & 2) *lose* electrons from their outer shell to form **positive ions** — e.g. sodium (2,8,1) loses 1 electron to become Na⁺ (2,8). Non-metal atoms (e.g. Group 7) *gain* electrons to fill their outer shell, forming **negative ions** — e.g. chlorine (2,8,7) gains 1 electron to become Cl⁻ (2,8,8). An **ion** is a charged particle formed when an atom loses or gains electrons.

## Section G: Ionic Compounds & Formulae

**Key ideas:** Ionic bonding is the strong *electrostatic attraction* between oppositely charged ions, e.g. Na⁺ and Cl⁻ in sodium chloride, NaCl. In any ionic compound, the total positive charge must **balance** the total negative charge. Magnesium (Mg²⁺) needs *two* Cl⁻ ions to balance its +2 charge, forming **MgCl₂**.

## Section H: Reactivity Trends

**Key ideas:** **Group 1** (lithium, sodium, potassium): reactivity *increases* down the group. The outer electron is further from the nucleus in bigger atoms, so it is held less strongly by the protons and is easier to lose. **Group 7** (fluorine, chlorine): reactivity *decreases* down the group. The electron being gained enters a shell closer to the nucleus in smaller atoms, so it is attracted more strongly.

## Section I: Why Atoms Bond

**Key ideas:** Atoms bond because they want a **full outer shell**, **greater stability**, and **lower energy**. They achieve this by **sharing**, **gaining**, or **losing** electrons. There are three main types of chemical bonding: **ionic** (electrons transferred), **covalent** (electrons shared), and **metallic** (electrons move freely between atoms).

## Section J: Covalent Bonding

**Key ideas:** Covalent bonds usually form between **two non-metals**. Instead of transferring electrons, the atoms **share** one or more pairs of electrons so that both achieve a full outer shell. E.g. two fluorine atoms each contribute one electron to a shared pair, forming F₂. Water (H₂O) has two covalent O–H bonds. In some molecules (e.g. SO₂) atoms share *two* pairs of electrons with each neighbour, forming a **double covalent bond**.

## Section K: Metallic Bonding

**Key ideas:** Metallic bonding happens only in **metals**. The outer-shell electrons are not held by any one atom — they move **freely** between all the metal atoms, often called a **"sea of electrons"**. This explains why metals are **shiny**, **conduct electricity**, and can be **bent or shaped** without breaking.

**E6** (3 marks)
