## Key Rules Recap

Read this page before starting. Everything in the test comes from these rules.

**Rational numbers** — can be written as a ratio of two integers a/b (b ≠ 0). Includes whole numbers, negative integers, fractions, percentages, terminating decimals and recurring decimals.

**Irrational numbers** — cannot be written as a ratio of two integers. The decimal goes on forever *without* repeating: π, √2, 0.1010010001…

**Surds** — a root that cannot be simplified to a rational number. √5 is a surd; √9 = 3 is not.

**Estimating roots** — find the perfect squares either side. 9 < 12 < 16, so 3 < √12 < 4.

**Laws of indices**

| Rule | Law | Example |
|------|-----|---------|
| Multiplying | aᵐ × aⁿ = a⁽ᵐ⁺ⁿ⁾ — **add** | x⁴ × x³ = x⁷ |
| Dividing | aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = a⁽ᵐ⁻ⁿ⁾ — **subtract** | a⁸ ÷ a³ = a⁵ |
| Power of a power | (aᵐ)ⁿ = a⁽ᵐˣⁿ⁾ — **multiply** | (x³)⁴ = x¹² |
| Zero exponent | a⁰ = 1 (a ≠ 0) | 7⁰ = 1 |
| Negative exponent | a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ — **reciprocal** | 2⁻³ = 1/8 |

**Standard form** — a × 10ᵇ, where a is between 1 and 9.9̇ and b is a whole number (either can be negative). Big numbers give a positive b; numbers below 1 give a negative b.

**Substituting** — replace each letter with its given value, then follow BIDMAS/BODMAS.

**Constructing expressions** — translate a word phrase into algebra: "increased by" → +, "decreased by" → −, "n of something at $x each" → xn.

**Algebraic indices** — multiply/divide the coefficients separately from the letters, then apply the index laws above to the letters.

**Expanding brackets** — a(b + c) = ab + ac for a single bracket; for two brackets, multiply every term in the first by every term in the second (FOIL), then collect like terms.

**Simplifying algebraic fractions** — divide the numerator and denominator by their highest common factor (a shared number, letter, or both).

**Rearranging formulae** — use inverse operations to isolate the subject: undo addition/subtraction first, then multiplication/division.

# Part 1 · Rational & Irrational Numbers

## Section A: Rational Numbers

**Definition:** A rational number can be written as a **ratio of two integers**, a/b (where b ≠ 0). This includes whole numbers, negative integers, fractions, percentages, decimals that *terminate* (end), and decimals that *repeat* forever in a pattern.

**Examples:** 3 = 3/1 · 0.10 = 1/10 · 0.333... = 1/3 · -8 = -8/1 · 25% = 25/100

### Reference Groups

| Group 1 | Group 2 | Group 3 | Group 4 |
|---------|---------|---------|---------|
| 1       | -5      | 1/2     | π       |
| 2       | -8      | 3/4     | √2      |
| 3       | -12     | 5 2/5   | 0.1434... |

## Section B: Irrational Numbers

**Definition:** An irrational number **cannot** be written as a ratio of two integers. Its decimal expansion goes on forever *without* repeating in a pattern.

**Examples:** π = 3.14159..., square roots of non-perfect squares like √2, and decimals like 0.1010010001...

**Together, rational + irrational numbers make up the *real number system*** — every point on the number line is either one or the other, never both.

## Section C: Surds

**Definition:** A **surd** is a root of a number that **cannot be simplified to a rational number**. Roots of perfect squares (like √4 = 2) are *not* surds, because they simplify to a whole number.

**Example:** √2 is a surd (2 isn't a perfect square). √9 is not a surd, because it simplifies to the rational number 3.

## Section D: Irrational Numbers on a Number Line

**Method:** Use the decimal expansion to estimate where an irrational number sits. Find two consecutive perfect squares either side of the number under the root, then compare.

**Example:** √12 ≈ 3.4641... — since 9 < 12 < 16, and √9 = 3, √16 = 4, we know √12 lies between 3 and 4 (closer to 3.5).

### Number Line Reference

```
0     1     2     3     4     5     6
|-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|
                    ↑
                 √12 ≈ 3.46
```

## Section E: Multiplying Powers

**Law:** When multiplying powers with the same base, you **add** the exponents: a^m × a^n = a^(m+n).

**Example:** x³ × x⁵ = x^(3+5) = x⁸ or 2⁴ × 2² = 2^(4+2) = 2⁶ = 64

## Section F: Dividing Powers

**Law:** When dividing powers with the same base, you **subtract** the exponents: a^m ÷ a^n = a^(m-n).

**Example:** x⁷ ÷ x² = x^(7-2) = x⁵ or 5⁶ ÷ 5² = 5^(6-2) = 5⁴ = 625

## Section G: Power of a Power

**Law:** When raising a power to another power, you **multiply** the exponents: (a^m)^n = a^(m×n).

**Example:** (x³)⁴ = x^(3×4) = x¹² or (2⁵)² = 2^(5×2) = 2¹⁰ = 1024

## Section H: Negative and Zero Exponents

**Zero exponent:** Any non-zero number to the power of 0 is **1**: a⁰ = 1 (where a ≠ 0).

**Negative exponent:** A negative exponent means the **reciprocal**: a^(-n) = 1/(a^n).

**Example:** 2^(-3) = 1/2³ = 1/8

## Section I: Multi-Step Problems

## Section J: What is Standard Form?

**Definition:** Standard form writes a number as a × 10^b, where **a** is between 1 and 9.9… (can be negative) and **b** is a whole number (also can be negative).

**Example:** 3 × 10⁸ = 300,000,000

## Section K: Converting Large Numbers to Standard Form

**Method:** Keep dividing the number by 10 until you get a number between 1 and 9.9… — that's **a**. Count how many times you divided (how many places the decimal point moved left) — that's **b**.

**Example:** 4000 → 4 × 10³ (the decimal point moved 3 places left).

## Section L: Converting Small Numbers to Standard Form

**Method:** For numbers smaller than 1, the decimal point moves **right** to reach the first non-zero digit — that makes **b** negative.

**Example:** 0.002 → 2 × 10^(-3) (the point moved 3 places right).

## Section M: Converting from Standard Form

**Method:** The power of 10 tells you how many places to move the decimal point — **right** for a positive power (number gets bigger), **left** for a negative power (number gets smaller).

**Example:** 9 × 10⁴ = 90,000 and 8.7 × 10^(-3) = 0.0087

## Section N: Multiplying & Dividing in Standard Form

**Method:** Multiply/divide the front numbers together, and use the laws of indices on the powers of 10 (add for ×, subtract for ÷). If the front number ends up outside 1–9.9…, renormalise it and adjust the power of 10 to compensate.

**Example:** (7 × 10³) × (6 × 10¹⁰) = 42 × 10¹³ = 4.2 × 10¹⁴ — 42 isn't in range, so it becomes 4.2 and the power increases by 1.

**Method:** Replace each letter with its given value, then follow the order of operations (BIDMAS/BODMAS) — powers before multiplying, multiplying before adding or subtracting.

**Example:** If x = 3, evaluate 2x + 5 → 2(3) + 5 = 6 + 5 = **11**

## Section R: Expanding Products of Linear Expressions

**Method:** For a single bracket, multiply everything inside by the term outside: a(b + c) = ab + ac. For two brackets, multiply every term in the first bracket by every term in the second (FOIL), then collect like terms.

**Example:** (x + 2)(x + 3) = x² + 3x + 2x + 6 = **x² + 5x + 6**

## Section T: Deriving and Using Formulae

**Method:** To rearrange a formula, use inverse operations to isolate the letter you want as the subject — undo addition/subtraction first, then multiplication/division. To use a formula, substitute the given values and evaluate.

**Example:** A = ½bh, rearranged to make h the subject → **h = 2A / b**
