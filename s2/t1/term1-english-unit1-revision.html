Section A

The Sentence & Simple Sentences

Every sentence needs a subject (who/what is being discussed) and a predicate (the part containing the verb). A simple sentence contains only one subject and one predicate — but that subject or predicate can be compound (more than one person/place/thing, or more than one action). The four types: SS (single subject/single predicate), CS (compound subject/single predicate), SC (single subject/compound predicate), CC (compound subject/compound predicate).

A compound sentence joins two or more independent clauses. The first way to do this is with a coordinating conjunction — remember them with FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so. A comma usually goes before the conjunction, though it can be dropped if both clauses are short.

Compound Sentences — MOTH & Semicolons

A compound sentence can also be formed with a conjunctive adverb — remember four of them with MOTH: Moreover, Otherwise, Therefore, However. Punctuation rule: semicolon before, comma after the conjunctive adverb. A third way to join independent clauses is with only a semicolon — but only when the two clauses are very closely related.

Section D

Complex & Compound-Complex Sentences

A complex sentence has at least one independent clause plus at least one dependent clause, joined by a dependent marker (e.g. although, because, if, when, since, unless...). Comma rule: comma after the dependent clause if it comes first; no comma if it comes after the independent clause. A compound-complex sentence combines at least two independent clauses with one or more dependent clauses.

Section E

Reading Comprehension with P.E.E.E

P.E.E.E stands for Point (your main idea, directly answering the question), Evidence (a short, well-chosen quotation from the text), Explanation (how the evidence supports your point — don't just repeat the quotation), and Explore (zoom in on one word, phrase or technique and analyse its effect on the reader).
Sam stood outside the examination room. His notes shook in his hands. Everyone else appeared calm, but his thoughts crashed into one another. When his name was called, he took a slow breath and stepped forward.
Question used below: How does the writer present Sam's challenge?
